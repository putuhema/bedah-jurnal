Why do cats purr? The science of a mysterious sound

By Dr. Maya Hart

Cats purr at a frequency between 25 and 150 hertz, a range that some researchers believe can help bones heal. The sound is produced by the rapid twitching of the muscles in the larynx, which open and close the space between the vocal cords as the cat breathes in and out.

For a long time scientists assumed purring simply meant a cat was content. Recent studies show that cats also purr when they are injured, giving birth, or even close to death, which suggests the purr may be a way of calming themselves down.

A sound with a hidden message

Domestic cats have learned to add a high-pitched cry to their purr when they want food. Researchers call this the solicitation purr, and humans find it surprisingly hard to ignore, much like the cry of a human baby.

Big cats such as lions and tigers cannot purr continuously, because the bone structure in their throat is built for roaring instead. Cheetahs, however, purr just like house cats, which makes them a favourite example in textbooks.

Whatever the reason, the purr remains one of the most studied and least understood sounds in the animal kingdom. Read more in the original study.
